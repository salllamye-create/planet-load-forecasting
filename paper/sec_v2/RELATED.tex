\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Load forecasting in power systems}
Operational load forecasting has long been built on explicit calendar structure: regression on
hour-of-day, day-of-week and holiday indicators interacted with a smooth nonlinear temperature
response is the backbone of practice and of the models that win forecasting competitions
\cite{hong2016gefcom,hong2016tutorial,wang2016recency,hong2019gefcom2017}, and the
comparative studies of the statistical era already found that seasonal exponential smoothing
and regression with a well-chosen weather input are hard to beat on European national load
\cite{taylor2007stlf}. Which weather to feed such a model is itself a modelling decision
\cite{hong2015weather}, which is why the weather representation is one of the sensitivity
analyses of this paper. Adaptivity after deployment is handled in practice by re-estimation:
the adaptive additive models and state-space corrections that tracked French demand through
the 2020 lockdown are the clearest recent example \cite{obst2021covid}, and the monthly refit
of our regression baseline and of the day-ahead comparison follows that practice.
Gradient-boosted trees on engineered lag, calendar and weather features are the modern
workhorse \cite{friedman2001gbm}. Deep learning entered the field through recurrent
architectures \cite{kong2019lstm}, probabilistic sequence-to-sequence forecasters
\cite{salinas2020deepar,wen2017mqrnn} and deep residual networks \cite{chen2019resnet}; recent
surveys \cite{hong2020review,eaai2025survey,haben2021lv} document a proliferation of hybrid
architectures, and their reported gains are difficult to compare because inputs, splits and
tuning budgets differ from paper to paper, a diagnosis made in the same terms for electricity
price forecasting \cite{lago2021epf}. Benchmark studies that hold the protocol fixed
\cite{fets2026,household2026} find that the ordering of methods changes with the task, the
sampling rate and the covariates supplied. This paper can be read as putting the calendar
regression of classical practice back inside a deep network, solved per instance and in closed
form, and as a benchmark in which the information given to every method is the same by
construction.

\subsection{Long-horizon deep forecasting and exogenous inputs}
Informer \cite{zhou2021informer} established the long-horizon benchmark protocol; Autoformer
\cite{wu2021autoformer} and FEDformer \cite{zhou2022fedformer} added decomposition and
frequency-domain attention; Zeng \emph{et al.} \cite{zeng2023dlinear} showed that a single
linear layer is competitive, prompting strong linear and frequency-interpolation models
\cite{li2023rlinear,xu2024fits}. PatchTST \cite{nie2023patchtst} recovered the Transformer's
advantage through patching and channel independence, iTransformer \cite{liu2024itransformer}
by treating variates as tokens, and TimeMixer \cite{wang2024timemixer} through multi-scale
mixing. Basis-expansion models---N-BEATS \cite{oreshkin2020nbeats}, N-HiTS
\cite{challu2023nhits}---forecast by combining basis functions, but their bases are indexed by
position within the window and their coefficients are produced by a network, whereas ours are
indexed by wall-clock time and obtained by solving a regularised least-squares problem. The
closest relative is DeepTime \cite{woo2023deeptime}, which also fits a ridge regression in
closed form per window, over meta-learned time-index features: our design fixes the features
to the calendar (harmonics of the day, week and year with weekend and holiday day-types),
weights the observations, and lets a residual network model what the calendar does not,
which is the combination the ablation tests. CycleNet \cite{lin2024cyclenet} learns one
periodic template per series indexed by cycle position and forecasts the residual, a learned
and global analogue of the per-window estimate used here; the ES-RNN that won the M4
competition \cite{smyl2020esrnn} combines per-series exponential-smoothing seasonality with a
global recurrent network and is the earlier hybrid of a classical seasonal state and a deep
residual model; dynamic harmonic regression \cite{young1999dhr} and Prophet
\cite{taylor2018prophet} are the statistical ancestors, harmonic regressors on calendar time
with holiday terms and slowly varying coefficients. The classical forms of adaptation that the
per-window solve replaces are regression with autoregressive errors, the dynamic regression of
the forecasting textbooks \cite{hyndman2021fpp3}, and the hourly state-space models with
time-varying regression coefficients built for French national load \cite{dordonnat2008load};
the monthly-refit and AR-error variants of Hong's regression in the benchmark stand for that
family.

Known-future covariates are consumed natively by a smaller family: the Temporal Fusion
Transformer \cite{lim2021tft}, N-BEATSx \cite{olivares2023nbeatsx}, TiDE \cite{das2023tide},
TSMixer with auxiliary inputs \cite{chen2023tsmixer}, TimeXer \cite{wang2024timexer} and
XLinear \cite{xlinear2026}. Most of the strongest long-horizon models cannot accept them at
all. We compare both families under identical inputs by giving every model without a native
covariate interface one shared exogenous head (Section~\ref{sec:head}), the same head our
own model uses.

\subsection{Time-series foundation models}
Pre-trained forecasters such as Chronos \cite{ansari2024chronos,chronos2}, TimesFM
\cite{das2024timesfm} and Moirai \cite{woo2024moirai} forecast zero-shot, and the latest
generation accepts covariates. Benchmarks on energy load find them competitive with
task-specific trained models when covariates are supplied \cite{fets2026}, and with
statistical baselines at day-ahead horizons on modest hardware \cite{tsfm2026energy}. They are reference points rather than
competitors under our protocol---their training corpora may contain the public electricity
series used here---and we report them as such.

\subsection{Distribution shift and test-time adaptation}
Reversible instance normalisation \cite{kim2022revin} and non-stationary Transformers
\cite{liu2022nonstationary} remove first- and second-moment shift at the input; slice-wise and
frequency-adaptive normalisation \cite{liu2023san,ye2024fan} and Dish-TS \cite{fan2023dishts}
extend the idea to periodic and horizon-dependent components, removing per-instance dominant
frequencies before the backbone and restoring them after it, which is the window-relative
counterpart of the calendar-indexed basis used here. Online and continual learning methods
\cite{pham2023fsnet,wen2023onenet} and test-time adaptation \cite{kim2025tta} continue to
update parameters after deployment. The adaptive part of our model is instead a convex problem
with a closed-form solution, so adaptation is exact, instantaneous and cannot diverge, at the
price of adapting only the calendar coefficients. Differentiable optimisation layers of this
general form have been studied elsewhere \cite{amos2017optnet,bertinetto2019closedform,%
gould2022ddn}; to our knowledge they have not been used to carry calendar structure in
forecasting.

\subsection{Evaluation and calibration}
Operational reserve decisions need distributions \cite{hong2016tutorial,gneiting2007scoring}.
Conformalised quantile regression \cite{romano2019cqr} supplies finite-sample coverage under
exchangeability and adaptive conformal inference \cite{gibbs2021aci} restores it under drift;
its use on time series, including the choice of step size and the delay with which outcomes
become available, is studied in \cite{zaffran2022aci}. Calibrated quantiles that cross are
rearranged \cite{chernozhukov2010rearrangement}, and a quantile set is scored by
quantile-weighted rules \cite{gneiting2011quantile}, of which the quantile-weighted CRPS
approximation used here is the uniformly weighted case over the emitted levels. For point
forecasts, the Diebold--Mariano test \cite{diebold1995dm,harvey1997dm} compares two
methods; when many methods are compared across many cells the Model Confidence Set
\cite{hansen2011mcs} identifies the subset not significantly worse than the best while
controlling the family-wise error. We use both, with a multiple-comparison correction.
